How to Store Printed Blankets Long Term

By David Chen • March 26, 2024 • 10 min read

Short answer: Store printed blankets by rolling the large sizes, folding only the small ones, and keeping them in a cool dry place with cedar rather than plastic. Avoid attics and basements where humidity swings, rotate seasonal pieces so the print is not creased in one spot for years, and never stack heavy items on top of the rolled fabric. Moths and damp are the two real risks to plan around.

Bottom line: Roll the 50x60 and 60x80 sizes around a tube, store the 30x40 flat with cedar, and check the pile every season when you swap blankets.

Why storage matters for printed fabric

A printed throw is not like a plain wool blanket you can shove in a bin and forget. The image sits in the fibre, and a hard fold held for years can leave a line the print never fully loses. The good news from our wash tests is that the colours are stable; the risk is mechanical, from creasing and damp, not from the dye fading in the dark.

Storage is also about the moment you bring it back out. A blanket pulled from a mildewed basement or a moth-eaten shelf is a disappointment, not a keepsake, and the damage is often permanent by then. A few minutes of setup when you put it away is what keeps the print looking new the next time it comes off the shelf.

The second reason storage pays off is sentiment. A family photo throw is a record as much as a comfort, and a stained or creased one tells the wrong story when it finally comes out. Treating the print as the document it is changes how you store it, from a heap in a cupboard to a rolled, tagged, checked piece. The small effort keeps the memory readable for the people who were not in the room when it was made, and it is the difference between a keepsake handed down and a relic nobody mentions.

Folding versus rolling

Roll the 50x60 and 60x80 sizes around a wide tube, because folding large prints leaves a hard crease across the faces that can weaken the image over time. Use a tube at least a few inches across so the fabric is not bent tight, and wrap a clean cotton cloth between the tube and the print so nothing sticks or marks the surface.

Fold only the 30x40 size, and even then refold it along a new line each season so the same spot does not thin or set. Keep every roll loose enough that the fabric is not stretched, because tension is what causes a permanent wave in the print. The small size is the one that tolerates a flat stack without complaint.

Humidity, moths, and cedar

Damp is the quiet enemy. A sealed plastic bag traps the moisture that flees from the fibre and grows mildew, especially in a warm room, while a breathable cotton bag lets air move and the print stays dry. Keep the storage area cool and steady, because swings in humidity are harder on fabric than a constant mild temperature.

Moths go for natural fibres, and cedar is the old fix that still works. A cedar block or bag in the bin repels them and keeps the smell musty-free, without the chemical sting of mothballs that the fabric holds for weeks. Check the blocks each season and swap them when the scent fades, because spent cedar stops protecting the pile.

Seasonal rotation to avoid creasing

Rotate with the seasons, about four times a year, so no crease sets in one place for months on end. Bring the current season's piece out and reroll or refold the ones going away along a fresh line. The handling is light, and the payoff is a print that does not develop a permanent band from years spent in a single position on a shelf.

Rotation also gives you a built-in inspection. Each swap is a chance to check for any sign of damp or a moth, which is far easier to fix early than after a year of silence. If you only store one blanket, the same rule applies: unroll it, air it, and reroll on a new line rather than leaving it be until next winter.

What not to do

The mistakes are predictable. Plastic bags trap moisture, basements stay damp, attics swing hot and cold, and heavy boxes on top of a roll press a flat spot into the print that may not recover. Mothballs leave a smell the fabric keeps long after the pests are gone, and folding the large sizes the same way every year bakes a crease into the faces.

None of these are hard to avoid once you know the pattern. The rule of thumb is simple: keep the fabric dry, keep it loose, keep it aired, and keep it off the floor. The wash test we ran shows the print is tough; the storage is where most real damage actually happens, and it is all preventable with a little routine.

  • Do not store blankets in plastic bags that trap moisture
  • Do not keep them in a damp basement or a hot attic
  • Do not pile heavy boxes on top of rolled blankets
  • Do not use mothballs that leave a smell the print holds
  • Do not fold the large sizes the same way every year

A simple storage setup

You do not need much. A wide tube, a cotton bag, a cedar block, and a shelf inside the home cover nearly every case. Roll the big sizes, fold the small one with cedar beside it, and label the season so the swap is a two-minute job rather than a search. The custom blankets page lists the sizes if you are sorting a mixed pile.

If a blanket comes back from a season looking creased, a cool iron on the back side and a day flat usually relaxes it, and the pillow care guide has the same logic for printed fabric around the house. Store with care once and the print stays sharp for the next generation, which is the whole point of keeping a family photo throw in the first place.

When to retire a stored blanket

Even with good storage, a printed throw has a natural life, and knowing when to retire one saves you from handing down a faded relic. If the fabric has thinned, the edge has frayed past a clean line, or the print has a permanent crease you cannot relax, the piece has done its job and it is time to step it down to a pet bed or a craft project rather than the wall where guests will notice.

Retiring does not mean binning. A blanket with a soft print but sound fabric still works as a picnic or a car throw, where daily use matters more than a perfect image. Keep the best pieces in rotation and let the worn ones take the rough jobs, so the keepsake stays special while nothing goes to waste. The wash test we ran shows the fibre outlasts the colour, so wear, not fade, is usually the real limit on a stored piece.

If you are storing for the next generation, write the year and the names on a tag pinned to the tube, because a blanket pulled out in twenty years carries more weight when the story travels with it. Storage is the easy part; remembering why the photo mattered is the part that needs a note. A dated tag turns a folded fleece into a document the family can actually read instead of guess at after everyone who was there has gone.

Check the bin once a season even when nothing is wrong, because the habit is what catches a damp corner or a spent cedar block before it becomes damage. Ten minutes every three months beats an hour of regret when a keepsake comes out musty. Frequently Asked Questions

Should I fold or roll a photo blanket for storage?

Roll the 50x60 and 60x80 sizes around a wide tube, because folding large prints leaves a hard crease that can crack the image over time. Fold only the 30x40 size, and refold it along a new line each season so the same spot does not weaken. Keep the roll loose enough that the fabric is not stretched tight across the tube.

How do I protect photo blankets from moths?

Use cedar blocks or a cedar bag in the storage bin rather than mothballs, which leave a smell the fabric holds for weeks. Cedar repels moths and keeps the bin from smelling musty. Check the blocks each season and swap them when the scent fades. Keep the blanket in a breathable cotton bag so air still moves through the fabric.

Is a basement or attic safe for blanket storage?

Usually not. Basements stay damp and attics swing hot and cold, and both are rough on printed fleece. A cool, dry closet or a shelf inside the home is safer. If you must use a basement, keep the blanket off the floor in a sealed bin with cedar and check it each season for any sign of mildew before it spreads.

How often should I rotate stored blankets?

Rotate with the seasons, about four times a year. Bring the current season's blanket out and refold or reroll the ones going away along a fresh line so no crease sets in one place. The handling is light and the payoff is a print that does not develop a permanent line or a faded band from years spent in one fixed position on a shelf.

Can I store a photo blanket in a plastic bag?

Not a sealed one. Plastic traps the moisture that flees from the fabric and can grow mildew, especially in a warm room. Use a breathable cotton storage bag or a bin with small vents, and add cedar for moths. If you use plastic, leave it open or poke holes so air moves and the print stays dry through the storage period.
